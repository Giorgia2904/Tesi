\chapter{Introduction}

The present dissertation emerges from a critical necessity to deconstruct and reevaluate the traditional narrative of the history of mathematics, which has long been characterized by a male-dominated perspective that portrays the discipline as an exclusive realm of male intellectual.
This biased representation not only distorts the historical record by erasing the significant contributions and intellectual resilience exhibited by women, but it also heavily impacts contemporary educational dynamics. 
Extensive research in psychology and sociology highlights how the systemic lack of female role models, paired with persistent and unaddressed cultural biases, creates invisible powerfull barriers that contribute to the phenomenon of stereotype threat.
Particularly during the delicate developmental transition of middle school, female students often develop heightened levels of mathematics anxiety and lower academic self-efficacy, which frequently results in their self-selection out of STEM pathways -- not due to a genuine lack of aptitude, but as an ego-protective response to restrictive societal expectations.

Within this context, the present study explores an important question: can the history of women in mathematics be used not just to correct historical oversights, but also as a valuable teaching tool to challenge gender stereotypes and reshape students' views on mathematics? To answer this, the dissertation has two main goals. The first is historical and cultural: to highlight the trajectories and contributions of women mathematicians whose work is often overlooked in the primary narratives of mathematics. The second goal focuses on education: to create and test a school-based intervention that uses female role models to help address beliefs related to stereotypes in the classroom. In this way, the dissertation connects the history of mathematics with theories about motivation, self-belief, and identity, suggesting a combined approach to teaching mathematics.

The historical part of this study is not just a commemoration of the past. Instead, it aims to show that the lack of women in mathematics comes from structural, institutional, and cultural barriers, not from any differences in intelligence. Understanding these past experiences serves two purposes: it challenges a misleading view of who can be a mathematical genius and also offers teaching tools that can help change students' ideas about who can be part of the mathematical community. From this viewpoint, the history of mathematics is more than just a background story; it acts as a teaching tool for addressing the social beliefs that still influence how students see the subject.

The framework of this dissertation focuses on two main theories: Eccles' Expectancy-Value Theory and Bandura's Social Cognitive Theory. These frameworks provide a theoretical lens through which gendered differences in mathematical attitudes and beliefs can be interpreted. Rather than seeing these differences as something fixed or natural, they can be seen as the result of various influences, including how students view themselves, how much they value math tasks, social expectations, and their past experiences with success or failure.
In this context, important ideas like math anxiety, self-efficacy, and the endorsement of stereotypes play a key role in how students shape their identities in math. This is especially true during early adolescence, a time when comparing oneself to peers, feeling pressure from evaluations, and socializing around gender roles become more intense.

Building on these historical and theoretical foundations, the empirical component of the dissertation investigates whether a narrative-based intervention featuring female mathematical role models can lead to measurable changes in students' beliefs and attitudes toward mathematics. The study was conducted in December 2025 in several lower secondary school classes in Trieste and involved 362 students across three grade levels. Utilizing a quasi-experimental design, the intervention aimed to assess short-term changes in four key dimensions: mathematics anxiety, mathematics self-efficacy, attitudes toward mathematics, and the endorsement of gender stereotypes. By doing so, the study seeks to explore whether exposing students to counter-stereotypical mathematical narratives can help reduce the psychological burden associated with exclusionary representations of the discipline.

A significant portion of this dissertation is dedicated to the examination of Maryam Mirzakhani, whose life and contributions are analyzed as a particularly compelling case study. As the inaugural female recipient of the Fields Medal, Mirzakhani holds a unique symbolic and intellectual status that aligns closely with the objectives of this research. Her career trajectory serves as a powerful counterexample to prevalent stereotypes regarding mathematical aptitude. Additionally, her contributions to hyperbolic geometry, Teichmüller theory, and dynamical systems provide a platform for showcasing advanced mathematical creativity through the narrative of a woman whose accomplishments have significantly influenced modern mathematics. Consequently, her inclusion in this dissertation fulfills both a historical and pedagogical role, facilitating the recovery of women's contributions and fostering a reimagining of mathematical excellence in a more inclusive context.


The dissertation is organized into six chapters. Chapter 1 examines the historical trajectories of women in mathematics, spanning from antiquity to the twentieth century. It highlights the institutional and cultural barriers that contributed to their exclusion, while also acknowledging the significant intellectual contributions they made to the discipline. Chapter 2 introduces the socio-cognitive framework that supports the study, with a focus on expectancy-value processes, self-efficacy, mathematics anxiety, and stereotype threat. Chapter 3 explores Maryam Mirzakhani as a contemporary figure, using her story to investigate issues related to mathematical creativity, representation, and gender in depth. Chapter 4 outlines the design of the field study and the methodological choices that informed the school-based intervention conducted in Trieste. Chapter 5 presents the statistical analyses and empirical findings of the study. Finally, Chapter 6 discusses these results within the context of the theoretical framework and reflects on their implications for promoting inclusive mathematics education.

Ultimately, this dissertation asserts that uncovering the histories of women in mathematics is not merely a matter of historical justice; it is also an important educational strategy for challenging exclusionary representations within the discipline. By integrating historical reconstruction, socio-cognitive theory, and empirical research conducted in classrooms, this study aims to demonstrate that mathematics can be taught and understood as an accessible, creative, and universally human endeavor.
