\chapter{Women in Mathematics: A History of Exclusion and Achievement}\label{chap:Historychap}

\section{Introduction: Reclaiming the Historical Narrative}
The traditional narrative of the history of mathematics, which is deeply rooted in global tradition, has often been presented as a lineage of male genius, stretching from figures like Euclid and Archimedes to Newton and Gauss. This portrayal reflects centuries of systemic exclusion, where women were legally or socially denied access to formal education, scientific patronage, institutional appointments, and publication—the very avenues for recognition in mathematics (\cite{Alic86}). This marginalization not only resulted in a loss of potential talent but also created an incomplete and distorted historical record, obscuring the immense creativity and perseverance that women exhibited in engaging with the discipline.

A thorough historical analysis is undertaken to address this topic. The focus is on the lives, intellectual environments, and specific scientific contributions of pioneering women who, through remarkable perseverance, strategic navigation, or sheer lettered vigor, succeeded in making foundational contributions to the subject. Organized chronologically, the discussion traces the evolution of female involvement from the private philosophical schools of antiquity to the contemporary university system, while emphasizing the specific social, political, and institutional barriers encountered by each woman. By detailing their struggles, significant breakthroughs, and the enduring stories of their resistance, the aim is to fully integrate these personalitiess into the primary historical narrative of mathematics, illustrating that their achievements often resulted from overcoming challenges largely absent for their male counterparts.

The emphasis will be on the unique challenges faced in various eras: the quest for philosophical credibility in antiquity; the use of pseudonyms and informal support during the Enlightenment; the urgent demand for formal qualifications and professional legitimacy in the 19th century; and the struggle against academic sexism and political oppression in the 20th century. By highlighting the context of their lives, we not only honor their brilliance but also deepen our understanding of the significant cultural influences that have shaped the field of mathematics.

\section{Antiquity: Authority, Pedagogy, and Persecution}
The earliest documented contributions of women to mathematics did not arise from formal academies, which were absent in the modern sense, but rather from philosophical and family-based schools. This intellectual freedom frequently stemmed from exceptional privilege or close associations with prominent male scholars, which made the continuation of such legacies quite precarious.

\subsection{Hypatia of Alexandria: the Price of Public Intellect}
Hypatia (c. 360--415 AD) is widely acknowledged as the first female mathematician whose life and contributions are well-documented. She thrived in late Roman Alexandria, the final stronghold of classical Hellenistic learning, and was the daughter of Theon, a prominent mathematician and philosopher affiliated with the Museum (\cite{Waithe1987}).

\subsubsection{Theon's Legacy: Education and Textual Preservation} Hypatia benefited from a unique advantage: a thorough education under the direct guidance of her father Theon. Her academic prowess encompassed mathematics, astronomy, and philosophy. Her primary focus was on the important tasks of preservation and teaching. She devoted herself to editing, clarifying, and expanding upon the foundational texts of Greek mathematics. Notably, she worked on Diophantus's Arithmetica (advanced algebra) and Apollonius's Conics (geometry). In an era marked by literary decline, her efforts were crucial in making these complex works accessible to the next generation of scholars (\cite{Snyder2000}).
\subsubsection{Civic Power: Advisory Role and Public Discourse}
Hypatia of Alexandria is an example of a unique intersection of intellectual prowess and public authority in an era characterized by gendered constraints on women's roles. Rather than introducing groundbreaking theorems, her significance is rooted in her formidable presence as a public scientist. As the head of the Neoplatonic school, Hypatia offered instruction in mixed-gender and multi-religious classes, drawing students from across the Roman Empire. Moreover, her role as a political advisor to the Roman Prefect Orestes underscores her influence beyond academia. Historical accounts indicate that her ability to "appear in public before the chief magistrates" was an unprecedented feat for women of her time (\cite{Osen1974}). Hypatia's authority derived from her comprehensive knowledge and exceptional rhetorical abilities, marking her as a pivotal character in both scholarly and civic spheres.
\subsubsection{The Symbolic Fall: Persecution and the End of Classical Learning}
Hypatia's prominence ultimately became her fatal flaw. As Alexandria was consumed by a power struggle between the secular authority (Orestes) and the new religious authority (Bishop Cyril), Hypatia, a pagan philosopher advising the Prefect, was seen as a threat. In 415 AD, she was brutally attacked and killed by a Christian mob. This shocking event is not just a personal tragedy but is often cited as a symbolic end to the flourishing of classical, pagan scholarship in Alexandria, and a chilling testament to the extreme vulnerability of a woman who dared to occupy a position of public power (\cite{Dzielska1995}). Her death sent a clear message: for a woman, public scholarship could be an act of dangerous transgression.

\section{The Enlightenment: Patronage and Conceptual Breakthroughs}
Centuries passed before women gained again public recognition in mathematics. The environment shifted from philosophical schools to the wealthy salons and courts of the Enlightenment, where high society and intellect intertwined.
    \subsection{Maria Gaetana Agnesi: the Symbolic Professorship}
    Maria Gaetana Agnesi (1718-1799) is one clear example of succces and limitations of the privilege necessary for female scholarship in the 18th century.
    \begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{img/Ch1/Agnesi.jpg}
    \caption{\textit{Portrait of Maria Gaetana Agnesi}, by Maria Longhi (19th century).}
    \label{fig:agnesi}
    \end{figure}
        \subsubsection{Paternal Patronage: The Milanese \textit{Conversazioni}}
        She was the eldest of 21 children from a prominent Milanese silk merchant family, and her figure exemplifies the intersection of birthright and industriousness in the development of academic capabilities. Recognized as a child prodigy, Agnesi demonstrated linguistic mastery by the age of nine, a testament to her early and rigorous education. Her father, keenly aware of her potential, facilitated her growth by organizing frequent intellectual conversations that provided her with a platform to engage in debates on philosophy and science (\cite{Mazzotti2007}). 
        \subsubsection{The Analytical Synthesis: \textit{Instituzioni} and the Mistranslation of the Curve}
        Agnesi's motivation for her greatest work, \textit{Instituzioni analitiche ad uso della gioventù italiana} (1748), was notably practical; it was conceived as a comprehensive textbook intended to aid her younger brothers in their studies. This book explores how Agnesi's privileged upbringing, intertwined with her strong work ethic to shape her contributions to mathematics and education in the 18th century. This two-volume treatise was a groundbreaking publication on differential and integral calculus, which synthesized the work of Newton and Leibniz, making some of the most advanced mathematics of the time more accessible. Because of its clarity and comprehensive purpose, it earned her wide fame and many translations across Europe.

        Another notable legacy of Maria Gaetana Agnesi is the cubic curve known as the "Witch of Agnesi," defined by the equation $x^2y = a^2(a - y)$. This curve is often featured in calculus textbooks and used in analytical geometry. The term, however, stems from a linguistic misunderstanding; Agnesi referred to it as "la versiera," meaning "turning" in 18th-century Italian, which sounds like "avversiera," one of the words for "witch" in Italian. In 1801, John Colson's translation mistakenly rendered it as the "Witch of Agnesi," a memorable yet inaccurate name. This highlights the challenges of translating mathematical concepts across cultures and the humorous errors that can arise (\cite{Kennedy1980}).

        \subsubsection{The Bologna Chair: Recognition vs. Professional Career}
        Her remarkable success led Pope Benedict XIV to appoint her as the chair of mathematics and natural philosophy at the University of Bologna in 1750. This recognition made her the first woman to hold a university chair in mathematics, representing a monumental and symbolic victory against the institutional impediments of the time. However, she likely chose not to take up the position, devoting her life instead to charitable work and religious piety. This decision underscores how even significant achievements were often hindered by personal and social obligations that diverted women from pursuing long-term professional careers (\cite{Mazzotti2007}). The position was open, but the path for women to consistently hold such a role was not truly forged until the next century.

\vspace{1em}

The institutionalization of science during the 18th and 19th centuries —characterized by the rise of scientific academies, specialized journals, and prominent universities— sharply defined and intensified the barriers against women. They were generally prohibited from enrolling in universities, presenting papers, or participating in scientific society meetings. As a result, women's contributions often occurred in obscurity, necessitating ingenious means of circumvention.

\subsection{Émilie du Châtelet: the Transgression}
Gabrielle Émilie Le Tonnelier de Breteuil, Marquise du Châtelet (1706--1749), was a pivotal symbol in the French Enlightenment. Denied formal access to institutions, she used her wealth and aristocratic status to cultivate her own intellectual environment, particularly at her château, where she engaged with her renowned lover, Voltaire. She struggled to attain recognition as both a philosopher and scientist in a society that confined aristocratic women to predominantly social roles.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{img/Ch1/DuChatelet.jpg}
    \caption{\textit{Portrait of Gabrielle Émilie Le Tonnelier de Breteuil, marquise Du Châtelet}, by Marianne Loir (18th century).}
    \label{fig:duchatelet}
\end{figure}
\subsubsection{Philosophical Exegesis: Correcting the Concept of Kinetic Energy}
Du Châtelet's most renowned mathematical work is her comprehensive translation of Isaac Newton's \textit{Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica}. However, her contributions extended well beyond mere translation; they encompass a significant physical and philosophical analysis. In her commentary, she provided her own insights into Newton's theories, defending them against the Cartesian perspectives that were prevalent in France. Notably, her key contribution to physics was the clarification of the concept of kinetic energy, establishing it as proportional to the square of the velocity ($E\propto mv^2$), rather than simply to the velocity ($E\propto mv$) as had been previously suggested by others (\cite{Zinsser2006}).
\subsubsection{Gendered Geography: The Disguise at the Café Gradot}
Du Châtelet faced both institutional and social restrictions, barred from the University of Paris and excluded from the intellectual circles of the Enlightenment. A notable example is her need to dress in male attire to attend Café Gradot, where leading scholars debated mathematics and philosophy (\cite{Sperling2017}). These cafés were essential for ideas exchange, yet women were strictly prohibited, forcing Du Châtelet to physically transgress gender norms for access. This disguise symbolizes the broader pseudonymity women employed, like Sophie Germain's alias "Monsieur Le Blanc," highlighting that the main obstacles to women's scientific contributions were not their brains but the gendered obstacles of 18th-century Parisian cultural life. Such actions illustrate the lengths women had to go to participate in discussions that advanced scientific thought.
\subsubsection{The Enduring Influence: Translation and the Dissemination of Newtonianism}
Her translation of Newton, published posthumously after her death from complications related to childbirth, remains the definitive French version and solidified her status as a key figure in the transmission of modern physical mathematics in Europe. Her brief yet brilliant career tragically highlights how the biological realities of womanhood, coupled with a lack of systemic support, often resulted in the premature end of female academic pursuits.


\section{The Age of Credentials: Fighting for Academic Legitimacy}

\subsection{Sophie Germain: Anonymity and the Birth of Number Theory}
Sophie Germain's (1776-1831) formative years were shaped by the tumult of the French Revolution, which, ironically, afforded her the solitude necessary for self-study. Confined indoors, she greedily explored the contents of her father's library, mastering subjects such as mathematics and Latin. Despite her parents' efforts to deter her —by confiscating her clothing and denying her heat and light— she persevered, studying by candlelight while wrapped in blankets (\cite{Perl1978}).

\subsubsection{The Ruse of "M. Le Blanc": Securing Mentorship from Lagrange}
Sophie Germain's use of the pseudonym "Monsieur Antoine-Auguste Le Blanc" was pivotal in her mathematical career, allowing her to gain recognition in a male-dominated field. Initially, she adopted this identity to submit papers and receive materials from the exclusive École Polytechnique.
Her early work on analysis was submitted to Joseph-Louis Lagrange, a leading individual at the École. Lagrange was so impressed with the rigor and originality that he requested a meeting with "Le Blanc." When he discovered that this brilliant correspondent was a self-taught young woman, he chose to support her rather than dismiss her as a fraud. This recognition transformed their relationship into one of personal patronage, providing Germain with invaluable feedback and direction. However, this support came at a cost: her academic trajectory depended entirely on his goodwill, lacking institutional equality.

The more dramatic revelation occurred years later with Carl Friedrich Gauss, known as the "Prince of Mathematicians." Their correspondence began after Germain, using the pseudonym Le Blanc, submitted her research on number theory to him. 

The ruse was revealed during the Napoleonic Wars when Germain, concerned for Gauss's safety during the French siege of Jena in 1806, contacted General Pernety to ensure his protection. When the general mentioned that he was acting on behalf of "Mlle. Le Blanc," Gauss was confused and demanded clarification. Germain then revealed her true identity, explaining her disguise was necessary due to "the prejudices that pursue the sciences with so much harshness when a woman presents herself."

Gauss's response on July 30, 1807, remains a powerful testament to her abilities (\cite{gray1978}). He wrote:
\begin{quote}
How can I describe my astonishment and admiration at seeing my esteemed correspondent Monsieur Le Blanc change into this celebrated person whom he presents as a brilliant example of what I would find difficult to believe. The love for the abstract sciences, in general, and for the mysteries of numbers in particular, is very rare... But when a person of the sex which, by our customs and prejudices, must encounter infinitely more obstacles than men to familiarize herself with these thorny researches, succeeds nevertheless in surmounting these obstacless and penetrating the most important of them, she must unquestionably have the noblest courage, extraordinary talent, and superior brilliance.
\end{quote}

This emotional validation from a leading mathematician confirmed that her work was exceptional and worthy of praise. It highlights how the greatest minds recognized that erudition transcended the barriers of sex.
\subsubsection{Validation: the Cost of Anonymity}

Despite her significant contributions to number theory, particularly in developing the theory of Sophie Germain primes related to Fermat's Last Theorem, her most notable external recognition stemmed from her research in elasticity theory. After two unsuccessful attempts, she received the prestigious prize from the French Academy of Sciences in 1816 for her paper on the vibration of elastic surfaces, making her the only woman to achieve this honor on her own merits. However, she was never awarded a seat at the Academy or granted an honorary degree, and she often had to depend on male colleagues to present her work. Her life serves as a crucial example of the struggle of a genius who lacked institutional support.

\vspace{1em}

The mid-19th century represented a significant turning point. As the modern, professional university system began to take shape in Europe, the informal patronage that had supported women like Germain became inadequate. True legitimacy now demanded formal academic credentials—namely, a Ph.D. and a university professorship. The difficulty evolved from merely gaining access to intellectual discourse to dismantling the conventional barriers that restricted participation.

\subsection{Sofya Kovalevskaya: The Ph.D. Barrier}
Sofya Vasilyevna Kovalevskaya (1850--1891) emerges as a monumental figure of her time. Her life is a testament to the passionate and resolute struggle for both a degree and a career. Her journey underscores the remarkable personal sacrifices that Russian women were compelled to make to pursue higher education abroad.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{img/Ch1/Kovalevskaya.png}
  \caption{\textit{Portrait of Sofia Kovalevskaya}, by Waldemar Dahllöf (1880--1885).}
  \label{fig:kovalevskaya}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{A Necessary Fiction: Marriage as a Passport for Exile and Study}
Russian universities were largely inaccessible to women, prompting Kovalevskaya to pursue her education in Germany, which was then the predominant center of mathematical research. To leave Russia without her father's consent, a young woman was legally required to demonstrate independence, a status that was typically achieved through marriage. In a notable yet pragmatic act of defiance against societal constraints, Sofya entered into a "fictitious marriage" with Vladimir Kovalevsky, a paleontology student, in 1868 (\cite{cooke1984}). This partnership, which enabled them both to travel to Heidelberg and subsequently Berlin, was a transactional arrangement —a means to access scholarship—that highlights the absurd legal barriers imposed on intellectually ambitious women.
\subsubsection{Breaking the Gate: Weierstrass's Tutelage and the Göttingen Ph.D}
Upon arriving in Germany, Kovalevskaya encountered her next challenge: the University of Berlin prohibited women from attending lectures. Undaunted, she sought the mentorship of Karl Weierstrass, one of the era's most distinguished mathematicians. Initially hesitant, Weierstrass quickly recognized her exceptional talent after reviewing her work. He agreed to provide her with private tutoring for four years, developing a personalized curriculum equivalent to full university enrollment.

The depth of their collaboration became evident when Weierstrass used his considerable influence to circumvent the university's regulations. In 1874, Kovalevskaya was awarded a Ph.D. in mathematics, summa cum laude, in absentia (without a public examination) from the University of Göttingen (\cite{koblitz1983}). She became the first woman in modern Europe to obtain a doctorate in mathematics, a remarkable achievement facilitated by a combination of private study, male support, and institutional exceptions.
\subsubsection{Triumph in Mechanics: The Bordin Prize and Professional Authority}
Despite holding a Ph.D., Kovalevskaya faced a decade of persistent rejection from European universities as she struggled to secure an academic position. Her professional breakthrough finally arrived in 1883 when she was appointed as a Privatdozent at Stockholm University, later becoming a full professor in 1889 —the first woman to attain a modern professorship in mathematics.

Her most notable scientific achievement is the Cauchy-Kovalevskaya Theorem, which addresses the existence and uniqueness of solutions to certain partial differential equations. Additionally, her significant work on the rotation of a rigid body about a fixed point garnered her the prestigious Bordin Prize from the French Academy of Sciences in 1888. The jury was so impressed by the originality and depth of her solution —one that introduced a previously unknown, integrable case to the equations of motion— that they doubled the prize money (\cite{cooke1984}). Kovalevskaya's success demonstrated that women could not only attain the highest academic credentials but also produce mathematics deserving of the world's most esteemed scientific honors.

\subsection{Mary Somerville: Synthesis, Exegesis, and the Royal Society}
While Kovalevskaya fought for institutional entry, Mary Somerville (1780--1872) took an alternative path by using her genius for clarity and synthesis to become the era's foremost popularizer and translator of advanced mathematical physics.

\subsubsection{Secret Studies: Popularizing Laplace's \textit{Mécanique Céleste}}
Born in Scotland, Somerville had no access to formal education. Similar to Germain, her scientific curiosity was often discouraged by her family. She had to study geometry and algebra in secret, utilizing disguised materials and rising before dawn to pursue her passion. 

Her key intellectual contribution lay in her talent for exegesis. Her translation of Pierre-Simon Laplace's five-volume work, \textit{Mécanique Céleste} (published in English as \textit{The Mechanism of the Heavens}), was not only a translation: it served as a simplified, chapter-by-chapter commentary. By making Laplace's complex, calculus-heavy text accessible to a broad English-speaking audience, she effectively laid the groundwork for the dissemination of modern Newtonian mechanics in Britain.

\subsubsection{Conceptual Leap: Inspiring Lovelace's Vision of Programmability}
Somerville's subsequent work, \textit{On the Connexion of the Physical Sciences} (1834), became a bestseller throughout Europe and was the first book to propose the possibility that a planet could perturb Uranus's orbit—a prediction that ultimately led to the discovery of Neptune (\cite{patterson1983}). Her significant influence on the next generation was widely recognized; in 1835, she was nominated, alongside Caroline Herschel, as the first female honorary member of the Royal Astronomical Society. Furthermore, her articulate explanation of Babbage's Analytical Engine and its potential inspired her contemporary, Ada Lovelace, to envision the device's future beyond mere calculation.

\section{Modern Mathematics: Systemic Sexism and Foundational Work}
\subsection{Emmy Noether: Algebraic Foundations}
Emmy Noether (1882--1935) is frequently regarded as one of the most noteworthy mathematical geniuses to emerge since the advent of higher education for women, as Albert Einstein himself stated in 1935 (\cite{reid1970}). Her contributions have profoundly transformed two principal domains: theoretical physics and abstract algebra.

    \label{fig:noether}

\subsubsection{The Göttingen Struggle: Habilitation and Hilbert's Defense}
Emmy Noether earned her Ph.D. from the University of Erlangen in 1907. However, her significant challenges unfolded at the University of Göttingen, which was the preeminent hub of mathematics at the time. Despite the staunch support of David Hilbert and Felix Klein, the mathematics faculty consistently denied her the right to Habilitation —the qualification required for university teaching— for nearly a decade. Their argument was steeped in sexism: "What will our soldiers think when they return to the university and discover they must learn at the feet of a woman?" (\cite{reid1970}). 

Hilbert's famous retort—"I do not see that the sex of the candidate is an argument against her admission as a Privatdozent. After all, we are a university, not a bathhouse", succinctly encapsulated the unreasonable opposition she encountered. Even after she was finally allowed to teach, she spent years lecturing without pay or an official title, often teaching courses that were formally listed under Hilbert's name. This situation highlights the enduring professional disenfranchisement that persisted even for someone of her talent.
\subsubsection{Dual Supremacy: Noether's Theorem and the Axiomatization of Algebra.}
Noether's brilliance was evident in her ability to synthesize and abstract, bringing together vast domains of mathematical thought. Her contributions can be classified into two key areas:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Noether's Theorem (Theoretical Physics).} Published in 1918, this theorem established one of the fundamental principles in science: the profound connection between symmetry in nature and conservation laws. For every continuous symmetry —such as those related to time or space— there exists a corresponding conserved quantity, like energy or momentum. This theorem continues to be a cornerstone of modern theoretical physics and cosmology.
    \item \textbf{Abstract Algebra.} Noether is recognized as the pioneer of a contemporary approach to algebra, particularly through her advancements in ring theory. Her introduction of the concept of the Noetherian ring (which bears her name) provided an essential axiomatic framework that simplified and unified extensive areas of algebraic geometry and topology (\cite{dick1981}).
\end{enumerate}
\subsubsection{Persecution and Exile: The Tragic End of a Foundational Career}
Noether's career, while exceptionally brilliant, was tragically cut short by politics. As a Jewish intellectual, she was abruptly dismissed from her position by the Nazi regime in 1933. This forced her to emigrate, ultimately finding refuge at Bryn Mawr College in the United States, where she continued her work until her untimely death in 1935. Her ordeal, culminating in forced exile, poignantly highlights the precariousness faced by even the most celebrated female scholars in the face of an oppressive political and social climate.

\subsection{War and the Code: Intersectional Barriers to Calculation}
The mid-20th century marked the emergence of a significant cohort of female mathematicians, often referred to as "human computers," who were instrumental in executing the intricate calculations necessary for military operations during wartime and subsequently for the endeavors of the space race. Despite their critical contributions, much of their work has been relegated to obscurity, remaining unrecognized and inadequately compensated. This phenomenon can be attributed to various institutional policies, particularly those about race, which have historically marginalized the contributions of these women in the field of mathematics.

In Britain, Mary Cartwright (1900--1998) gained institutional recognition as the first woman to be elected a Fellow of the Royal Society for her significant contributions to mathematics in 1947. During World War II, she collaborated with John Littlewood on research related to radar technology, focusing on the behavior of non-linear differential equations. This work culminated in the first identification and analysis of a complex phenomenon that would later be known as chaos theory, specifically the Littlewood-Cartwright theorem. Her research underscored the vital role of women's specialized expertise in mission-critical advancements (\cite{littlewood1957}).
\section{Conclusion: Beyond the Gendered Canon of Mathematics}
The lives documented herein —from Hypatia, the scholar who was murdered for her philosophical insights, to Noether, who faced exile despite her groundbreaking contributions to algebra— illustrate that the history of women in mathematics is characterized by formidable and unwavering intellectual resilience. They harnessed every possible advantage: patronage, pseudonyms, marriage, and sheer brilliance to navigate systemic exclusion.

Their experiences highlight that mathematical progress is not a smooth, linear journey, but rather one marked by significant cultural resistance. By fully integrating these "herstories" into the historical narrative, we not only rectify a long-standing omission but also acquire a more accurate and comprehensive understanding of the human endeavor that is mathematics. The path to the present has indeed been paved by those who dared to challenge the barriers.
