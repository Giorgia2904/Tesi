\chapter{Results}
This chapter presents the empirical findings derived from the data collection process to address the research questions and expectations of the study. The research aims to evaluate the short-term impact of a school-based educational intervention designed to challenge traditional gender stereotypes in mathematics through the introduction of female mathematicians to students.

To provide a comprehensive evaluation, the results are organized into sequential analytical sections. First, the preparation of the dataset and descriptive statistics are reported, followed by reliability analyses of the instruments. Subsequently, baseline comparisons at $T_0$ and the main inferential analyses—including mixed ANOVAs and paired-sample t-tests—are presented to explore the effects of the intervention over time. Finally, exploratory analyses regarding the effect of age or grade level, along with complementary qualitative data, are detailed to provide a complete interpretative context.

\section{Dataset preparation}
The initial sample consisted of 533 students enrolled in a lower secondary school in Trieste. Prior to statistical analyses, the dataset was screened to ensure data quality and compatibility with the within-subjects design.

After data cleaning procedures, the final analytical sample included 360 participants with complete pre-test and post-test data used for inferential analyses. The final analytical sample varied slightly across analyses due to listwise deletion of missing data.

\subsection{Data Cleaning}
Several exclusion criteria were applied to ensure the integrity of the repeated-measures design.

First, two participants who did not report gender were excluded from gender-based inferential analyses but retained in the overall dataset for descriptive purposes where applicable.

Second, cases with missing or non-matching identification codes across the two measurement waves (pre-test $T_0$ and post-test $T_1$), as well as cases with missing data due to absence at either administration point, were removed from the longitudinal dataset. This procedure ensured correct pairing of observations across time.

The final matched dataset after these exclusions was utilized for the subsequent paired-sample comparisons and mixed ANOVA models.

\subsection{Coding and Scoring}
Data preparation followed standard psychometric procedures.

Negatively worded items (e.g. ``I would prefer not to have math lessons'' in the Attitudes Toward Mathematics scale) were reverse-coded so that higher scores consistently reflected higher levels of the underlying construct.

Composite scores were then computed as the mean of item responses for each scale at both time points ($T_0$ and $T_1$), resulting in the following variables:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Math Anxiety ($AnxT0\_mean$, $AnxT1\_mean$) 
  \item Mathematics Self-Efficacy ($SelfT0\_mean$, $SelfT1\_mean$)
  \item Attitudes Toward Mathematics ($AttT0\_mean$, $AttT1\_mean$)
  \item Gender Stereotypes in Mathematics ($SterT0\_mean$, $SterT1\_mean$)
\end{itemize}

To assess change over time, difference scores were computed as $T_1 - T_0$ for each construct:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $Anxiety\_diff$
  \item $Self\_diff$
  \item $Att\_diff$
  \item $Ster\_diff$
\end{itemize}


\section{Sample Description}
The sample for the study comprises $N = 362$ students from a middle school (scuola secondaria di primo grado) located in the city center of Trieste, which serves a total population of approximately 533 students. The participants and the participating classes were selected using purposive sampling strategy based on teacher willingness and scheduling constraints. The sample is distributed across the three grade levels of the school: 
\begin{itemize}
\item Grade 6: 127 students
\item Grade 7: 121 students
\item Grade 8: 114 students
\end{itemize}

The sample was approximately balanced by gender. The final dataset included female (F) and male (M) students across all grade levels, with a small number of participants identifying outside the binary classification (O). \section{Reliability Analysis}
The internal consistency of the measurement instruments was assessed using Cronbach's alpha coefficients ($\alpha$) computed at baseline ($T_0$) for all four multi-item scales.
 Values equal to or above .70 are generally considered acceptable, while values above .80 indicate good internal consistency.

The analyses were conducted using Jamovi (version 2.7).
As reported in Table~\ref{tab:Cronbach_alpha}, all scales demonstrated good internal consistency at baseline:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Math Anxiety: $\alpha = 0.828$
  \item Mathematics Self-Efficacy: $\alpha = 0.821$
  \item Attitudes Toward Mathematics: $\alpha = 0.852$
  \item Gender Stereotypes: $\alpha = 0.815$
\end{itemize}

All values exceeded the commonly accepted threshold of .70, indicating satisfactory reliability for all constructs. This supports the use of composite scores for subsequent analyses.

Given that the same instruments were administered at both time points ($T_0$ and $T_1$), reliability was assessed at baseline under the assumption that internal consistency remains stable across short time intervals in the absence of structural changes to the instruments.

\section{Qualitative Results}
In addition to the quantitative data collected with the questionnaire, a qualitative summary was created using insights from the interactive introduction to the seminar, the questions asked from the students and the way they participated to the lecture. This observations aimed to assess students' initial understanding of mathematical figures, their personal reflections on the historical gender gap in the field, and their immediate responses to the biographical stories that were shared. 
\subsection{Baseline Awareness of Scientific and Mathematical Figures}
The first step of the inquiry required students to anonymously write down the names of famous mathematicians on a slip of paper (see \ref{fig:student_notes}). Their answers consistently featured traditional historical figures such as Newton, Galileo, Pythagoras, Einstein and more. While no female mathematicians included in standard history of mathematics were included, five female scientific figures emerged across the responses: Marie Curie, Rita Levi Montalcini; Samantha Cristoforetti, Margherita Hack, and Mileva Mari\'{c}, who was explicitly mentioned by students in connection to her marriage and collaboration with Albert Einstein.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{img/Ch5/names.png}
    \caption{A selection of notes written by the students during the seminar, showing the names of the mathematicians they learned about.}
    \label{fig:student_notes}
\end{figure}

During the second step, when asked what the listed names had in common, the students answered unanimously that they were all men, overlooking the few female exceptions previously noted. 

\subsection{Students' Explanation of Historical Gender Disparities}
The third prompt asked students to reflect on the reasons behind the low historical representation of women in mathematics. The responses can be divided into two main categories:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Structural and Legal Barriers:} A significant portion of the students, particularly female participants, explicitly identified external restrictions, noting that historically ``women didn't have rights'' or ``were not allowed to study''. Additionally, one male student stated that ``women were often behind the names of men who stole their research.''
  \item \textbf{Attitudinal and Social Factors:} Other responses focused on perceived internal factors or traditional social roles. For instance, one male student remarked that women were historically ``only used for cooking and procreating.'' Furthermore, a widespread sentiment expressed by both male and female students was that women in the past ``weren't interested'' or didn't like math." Throughout the inquiry, students framed these observations almost exclusively in the past tense, describing the gender gap as an issue confined to historical periods.
\end{itemize}
\subsection{Student Engagement}
The qualitative observation of the session revealed high levels of participation and questions from both male and female students. Two biographical accounts in particular recorded the highest engagement: 
\begin{itemize}
  \item Maria Gaetana Agnesi: Students expressed interest in her ability to speak seven languages alongside her mathematical work, which challenged their baseline descriptions of a mathematician's academic profile.
  \item Maryam Mirzakhani: Participants responded actively to the detail that the first female Fields Medalist initially wanted to become a writer during her childhood and had faced early academic underestimation from a teacher.
\end{itemize}

The seminar also led to broader questions, with a male student inquiring whether there were similar female figures in the history of physics. At the conclusion of the session, the dialogue shifted toward contemporary topics when two students asked the researcher: ``But is the problem solved today?''. This question allowed for a final discussion on how cultural frameworks and implicit stereotypes compare to past legal restrictions in modern educational paths.


\section{Summary of Findings}
In conclusion, the empirical assessment of the school-based educational intervention showcased notable trends across the measured constructs. The quantitative analysis revealed significant decreases in explicit gender stereotypes and mathematics anxiety, alongside an improvement in positive attitudes toward mathematics. However, mathematics self-efficacy remained unchanged over the one-week period. These outcomes were consistent across the sample, showing no significant differences related to participants' gender or grade level. 

Additionally, qualitative observations highlighted an initial inclination to link mathematics primarily with male historical figures and to consider women's historical underrepresentation as a result of a presumed lack of interest. The seminar encouraged engagement from both female and male students, with discussions sparked by the biographies of Maria Gaetana Agnesi and Maryam Mirzakhani, which extended the dialogue to current issues of gender representation in STEM fields.
