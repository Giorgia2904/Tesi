\chapter{The Present Research}\label{chap:PresentResearch}
Persistent gender disparities in mathematics continue to influence educational and career choices, often deterring girls from fully engaging with the subject. Prior research, as discussed in \ref{chap:SocialScience}, has demonstrated that gender stereotypes and stereotype threat adversely affect both performance and self-efficacy in mathematics. The present study examines whether a brief, school-based intervention can diminish stereotype endorsement and promote more positive affective and motivational outcomes among students.
\section{Research Questions}
The present study aims to answer the following research questions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item[RQ1.] \textbf{To what extent can the proposed intervention reduce gender stereotypes related to mathematics?} \\
    This question investigates whether exposing students to female role models can significantly diminish the endorsement of traditional beliefs regarding gender and mathematical ability within the school context.

    \item[RQ2.] \textbf{What is the impact of the intervention on female students' academic self-efficacy in mathematics?} \\
    The focus here is specifically on whether the intervention can enhance girls' confidence in their own mathematical skills. By providing relatable role models, the study aims to strengthen their belief in their capacity to succeed in math-related tasks.

    \item[RQ3.] \textbf{How does the school-based intervention affect mathematics anxiety levels among female students?} \\
    This question examines whether exposure to positive examples of women succeeding in mathematics can mitigate the apprehension girls often feel toward the subject. Reducing anxiety is crucial, as high levels of math anxiety are known to negatively affect performance.

    \item[RQ4.] \textbf{To what extent does the intervention improve students' general attitudes toward mathematics?} \\
    The final question explores whether the intervention can foster more positive emotional and motivational responses toward the subject, such as interest, enjoyment, and perceived utility, making mathematics appear more accessible to the entire student body.
\end{itemize}
Research Questions 2 and 3 focus specifically on female students, given the gendered nature of self-efficacy and math anxiety highlighted in the literature.

\section{Research Expectations}
Based on the literature review discussed in Chapter \ref{chap:SocialScience} and the objectives of the present intervention, a set of theoretically informed research expectations was formulated to guide the analyses. These expectations reflect patterns consistently reported in previous studies on gender stereotypes, role models, and affective factors in mathematics education. Given the exploratory nature of the study and the quasi-experimental pre--post design, they were not intended as confirmatory hypotheses, but rather as an interpretative framework for examining potential changes following the intervention.

\begin{itemize}
    \item[E1.] \textbf{Gender stereotypes are expected to decrease.} \\
    In line with prior research on the impact of female role models in counteracting gendered beliefs about mathematical ability, a reduction in gender-related stereotypes in mathematics is expected at the post-test ($T_1$) compared to the pre-test ($T_0$). In particular, exposure to successful women in mathematics is expected to challenge the perception of mathematics as a predominantly male domain.

    \item[E2.] \textbf{Female self-efficacy in mathematics is expected to increase.} \\
    It is expected that female students will show higher levels of mathematics self-efficacy following the intervention. Presenting relatable female role models and highlighting non-linear academic trajectories is expected to strengthen students’ confidence in their own mathematical abilities.

    \item[E3.] \textbf{Female math anxiety is expected to decrease.} \\
    Based on evidence linking stereotype reduction and role-model exposure to lower anxiety, female students are expected to report reduced levels of mathematics anxiety at the post-test compared to baseline. Framing mathematics through personal narratives and emphasizing accessibility is expected to mitigate feelings of threat associated with the subject.

    \item[E4.] \textbf{Attitudes toward mathematics are expected to improve.} \\
    Finally, a general improvement in students’ attitudes toward mathematics is expected following the seminar. This includes increased interest, perceived utility, and enjoyment of the subject, as a result of presenting mathematics as both socially relevant and attainable.
\end{itemize}

These research expectations provide a conceptual guide for interpreting pre--post differences observed in the measured constructs, without implying causal inference beyond the scope of the present design.Although inferential statistics were employed, they were used to explore statistically meaningful pre--post differences rather than to test strictly confirmatory hypotheses.


\section{Research Design}

This study employs a short-term quantitative quasi-experimental design using Likert-scale questionnaires. A within-group design is adopted, where the same students are assessed before and after a seminar featuring female mathematicians to evaluate changes in gender stereotypes, self-efficacy, math anxiety, and attitudes toward mathematics.
While the lack of a control group limits the ability to rule out maturation or history effects, as well as potential testing and social desirability effects, this design was deemed appropriate for a preliminary assessment of the intervention's short-term impact in a naturalistic school setting.


\section{Sample}
The research involved 362 students from a middle school (\textit{scuola secondaria di primo grado}) located in the city center of Trieste. The school serves a total population of approximately 533 students.

The school was selected using purposive sampling due to its specific characteristics and availability for the project. Similarly, the classes participating in the experiment were selected through purposive sampling, based on teacher willingness and scheduling compatibility.

The sample was distributed across three grade levels. Specifically, the participants included 127 students from Grade 6 (63 males, 62 females, and 2 who preferred not to disclose their gender), 121 students from Grade 7 (59 males and 62 females), and 114 students from Grade 8 (64 males and 50 females). Participants ranged in age from 10 to 14 years. It should be noted that while the two students who did not disclose their gender were included in the descriptive statistics of the total sample, they were excluded from the specific analyses comparing male and female groups.

\section{Procedure}
The study employed a pre- and post-test within-group quasi-experimental design to evaluate the effects of exposure to female role models in mathematics. The data collection and intervention took place in December 2025. The experimental procedure was divided into two distinct sessions to assess the retention of the intervention's effects.

The first session was conducted in the school's auditorium (aula magna), where the researcher personally welcomed groups of three or four classes at a time. This session lasted approximately 60 minutes and began with the administration of the pre-test questionnaire (approx. 20 minutes). Immediately following the data collection, the researcher delivered the intervention.

The seminar opened with an interactive three-step introduction designed to stimulate critical thinking. First, students were asked to write down the names of famous mathematicians on a slip of paper. Second, the researcher displayed images of well-known mathematicians, asking the students to identify what these figures had in common. Finally, the class was engaged in a discussion regarding the reasons behind the apparent lack of female representation in the field.

Following this introductory discussion, the researcher presented a chronological overview of female contributions to mathematics. The narrative covered figures such as Hypatia of Alexandria, the pioneers of the 18th and 19th centuries (Maria Gaetana Agnesi, Sophie Germain, and Sofya Kovalevskaya), and the \textit{Human Computers} of the 20th century. Throughout this historical section, the presentation explicitly highlighted the social and institutional obstacles these women faced, such as exclusion from universities and the necessity of using male pseudonyms to be taken seriously.

Particular emphasis was placed on the modern era and the life of Maryam Mirzakhani. The presentation detailed her achievement as the first female Fields Medalist. To foster a sense of relatability, the researcher highlighted her personal journey, noting that she originally dreamt of becoming a writer rather than a mathematician and mentioning her participation in the International Mathematical Olympiad. Finally, her complex theoretical work on curved surfaces was explained in an age-appropriate manner and directly linked to tangible modern applications such as GPS technology and Artificial Intelligence to demonstrate the practical utility of advanced mathematics.

The second session took place exactly one week after the intervention. In this final phase, participants completed the post-test questionnaire—identical to the pre-test—to assess changes in their perceptions over time.

\section{Instruments}\label{sec:Instruments}
Data were collected using a structured questionnaire. The instrument consisted of four main constructs, all measured using a five-point Likert scale, and was designed to capture students’ affective, cognitive, and motivational responses related to mathematics. The complete questionnaire is provided in \autoref{app:questionnaire}.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Math Anxiety. This construct assessed the anxiety students experience in learning and testing situations. The Abbreviated Math Anxiety Scale (AMAS; \cite{Hopko2003}) was used, employing the Italian translation and validation by \textcite{Caviola2017}. It included 9 items.

    \item Mathematics Self-Efficacy. This construct measured students’ perceived confidence in successfully performing mathematical tasks. The scale was based on the Academic Self-Efficacy instrument developed by \textcite{DiGiunta2013} and adapted for the context of middle school mathematics. It consisted of 10 items covering general confidence in math-related skills.

    \item Attitudes Toward Mathematics. This construct explored students’ general emotional and motivational relationship with mathematics, including interest, enjoyment, and perceived utility. The items were inspired by the Fennema-Sherman Mathematics Attitudes Scales \cite{FennemaSherman1976}. A subset of the original items (8 items) was selected to fit the middle school context and the scope of the present study. 

    \item Gender Stereotypes in Mathematics. This construct assessed students’ beliefs regarding gender and mathematics. It included perceptions of male advantage and personal anxiety about confirming gender stereotypes. The 9 items were developed based on \textcite{Bedynska2021} and \textcite{Dersch2022}.
\end{itemize}

All items were rated on a five-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating higher levels of the measured construct. The questionnaire was administered in paper format under standardized conditions to ensure reliability and comparability across participants.



\section{Data Collection}
The administration of the questionnaires was conducted using a paper-and-pencil format to minimize digital distractions and ensure standardized conditions for all participants. During both the pre-test and post-test sessions, the researcher supervised the activity with the support of the class teachers, ensuring that students completed the surveys individually and without sharing answers.

To guarantee anonymity while enabling the within-subject matching of pre- and post-test responses, a self-generated alphanumeric code system was employed. At the beginning of the first session, participants were guided to create a unique ID (based on non-identifying personal cues) which they reported on both questionnaires. This procedure allowed the researcher to link the data points ($T_0$ and $T_1$) for statistical analysis without collecting or storing personally identifiable information.
All collected materials were retrieved immediately after administration and stored in a secure location, accessible only to the researcher, in compliance with data protection regulations.


\section{Data Analysis}

Data management and preliminary cleaning were performed using Microsoft Excel. The dataset was first screened to ensure that each participant had a matching pre-test ($T_0$) and post-test ($T_1$) questionnaire. Participants who completed only one of the two sessions—due to absence on either the intervention day or the follow-up day—were excluded from the final dataset. Additionally, for analyses comparing gender groups, the two participants who did not disclose their gender were excluded to maintain binary comparability.

Negatively worded items (e.g., “I would prefer not to have math lessons” in the Attitudes scale) were reverse-coded so that higher scores consistently represented higher levels of the measured construct. Composite scores for each construct (Math Anxiety, Mathematics Self-Efficacy, Attitudes Toward Mathematics, and Gender Stereotypes) were calculated by averaging the respective items, preserving the original 1–5 metric.

The internal consistency of each construct was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha ($\alpha$), with reliability considered acceptable if $\alpha > .70$ according to standard guidelines.

To examine changes between pre-test and post-test scores, paired-samples t-tests were conducted for each construct. Given the approximately continuous nature of the composite scores and the robustness of the t-test to minor deviations from normality, parametric analyses were deemed appropriate. 

Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s $d$, with conventional thresholds of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 representing small, medium, and large effects, respectively. These analyses were intended to explore whether the observed differences aligned with the research expectations, rather than to test strict confirmatory hypotheses, in line with the exploratory nature of the study.


\section{Ethical Consideration}
This research was carried out in full compliance with recognized ethical principles for studies involving human participants, particularly minors. It followed the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki (1964), the European Code of Conduct for Research Integrity, and the Code of Ethics and Code of Conduct of the University of Trieste. Before the intervention and data collection began, formal authorization was obtained from the school's Principal, and informed consent was secured from both students and their parents or legal guardians. Participation was entirely voluntary, and students were clearly informed that they could withdraw from the study at any point without any negative consequences for their academic evaluation or school experience.

To protect anonymity and confidentiality, no personally identifying data were gathered. All questionnaire responses were coded, stored securely, and analyzed exclusively in aggregate form. Neither the identities of the students nor those of their schools were revealed in any reports, publications, or presentations arising from this research.

Particular attention was devoted to ensuring that all materials and procedures were age-appropriate and presented in a manner that avoided discomfort or confusion. Since the study addressed issues related to gender and mathematics, care was taken to ensure that no participant felt singled out or judged based on gender. Teachers were informed in advance about the goals and structure of the intervention and were present during all sessions to provide support if necessary. The seminar was framed positively, emphasizing inclusivity, diversity, and the accomplishments of women in mathematics, with the broader aim of creating a respectful and encouraging classroom environment.
