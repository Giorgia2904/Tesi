\chapter{Maryam Mirzakhani}\label{chap:MaryamBio}

\section{A biography}

Maryam Mirzakhani's biography tells the story of an extraordinary journey: from a young girl in Tehran who dreamed of becoming a writer, to a world-renowned mathematician whose name is firmly established in the history of mathematics. Her path was shaped by determination, and an intense pursuit of truth and beauty. Though cut short by illness, her story continues to inspire mathematicians and non-mathematicians. She remains not only a pioneer in her field but also a symbol of what can be achieved when talent and perseverance are nurtured by opportunity and courage.

\subsection{Early Life}
Maryam Mirzakhani was born on May 3rd, 1977, in Tehran, Iran, to a supportive and intellectually engaged family. Her father, Ahmad Mirzakhani, was an engineer, while her mother, Zahra Haghighi, dedicated herself to raising their four children. Growing up in the years following the Iranian Revolution and the Iran-Iraq War, Mirzakhani later reflected on her good fortune in reaching adolescence during a period of relative stability, as the country worked to rebuild its educational and cultural institutions (\cite{gibbons,series}).

As a child, Mirzakhani did not initially appear destined for a career in mathematics. She was an enthusiastic and avid reader with aspirations of becoming a writer, often spending countless hours absorbed in novels and biographies. She admired the remarkable lives of figures like Marie Curie and Helen Keller, whose stories inspired her with the belief that one could overcome challenges and make a significant impact on the world. Her vivid imagination even led her to create stories about ambitious heroines who traveled extensively and achieved great goals (\cite{gibbons}).

Her introduction to mathematics was partly inspired by her older brother, who one day shared the famous story of Carl Friedrich Gauss summing the numbers from 1 to 100. Although she was not able to solve the problem herself at the time, she was captivated by the idea that such funny shortcuts existed in mathematics. Such interest intensified when she began attending Farzanegan School, a prestigious institution under Iran's National Organization for Development of Exceptional Talents (NODET). At first, mathematics was not her strongest subject, and a teacher even suggested she lacked aptitude. However, the following year, she had a more encouraging teacher whose belief in her potential reignited her interest (\cite{gibbons}).

Alongside her close friend Roya Beheshti, Mirzakhani successfully convinced their school principal to allow them to train for the International Mathematical Olympiad (IMO). Prior to this, no Iranian girl had ever participated in the competition. In 1994, Mirzakhani won a gold medal at the IMO in Hong Kong, and the following year, she again earned gold in Toronto, with a perfect score. With this accomplishment, she became the first Iranian student, regardless of gender, to obtain such a distinction (\cite{gibbons,series}).

\subsection{University Studies and Academic Formation}
Following her success in international competitions, Mirzakhani enrolled at Sharif University of Technology in Tehran, one of the country's best institutions for science and engineering (\cite{series}). There, she thrived in a stimulating intellectual environment, enriched by problem-solving sessions and informal reading groups that contributed to her development. As an undergraduate, she published several research papers in combinatorics and graph theory, showing her early originality and determination (\cite{gibbons}).

In 1999, she earned a Bachelor of Science degree in Mathematics. That same year, she left Iran to pursue graduate studies in the United States. She enrolled at Harvard University, where she had the privilege of studying under Curtis T. McMullen, a Fields Medalist (\cite{gibbons,series}). Adapting to a new country and language posed challenges, yet she impressed her professors and peers with her curiosity, frequently posing questions in English while taking notes in Farsi.

Her doctoral years combined by both challenges and achievements. At first, she was not well-versed in certain advanced topics, but her determination and curiosity enabled her to overcome these difficulties. By the time she earned her Ph.D. in 2004, she had gained international fame for the depth and originality of her research (\cite{series}).

\subsection{Professional Career}
After completing her doctorate, Mirzakhani became a research fellow at the Clay Mathematics Institute while also serving as an assistant professor at Princeton University. In 2008, she joined the faculty at Stanford University, where she rapidly ascended the academic ranks and became a full professor by the age of thirty-one: an extraordinary accomplishment in a field known for its competitiveness and rigorous standards (\cite{gibbons,series}).

At Stanford, she distinguished herself not only as an exceptional researcher but also as a devoted educator and mentor. Students and colleagues noted her humility, warmth, and generosity. Although she was reserved in public settings, she became animated when discussing her ideas, often filling pages with intricate sketches as she developed her thoughts (\cite{zagrebnov}).

Her career was recognized by numerous honors. In 2009, she was honored with the American Mathematical Society's Blumenthal Award, followed by the Ruth Lyttle Satter Prize in 2013. The pinnacle of her recognition came in 2014 when she received the Fields Medal, the most prestigious award in mathematics. This distinguished her as both the first woman and the first Iranian to receive this award since its establishment in 1936 (\cite{zagrebnov}).

Despite such recognitions, Mirzakhani maintained a sense of modesty. She often highlighted the collaborative and playful aspects of mathematics, where persistence and imagination are vital (\cite{gibbons}).

\subsection{Personal Life}
Beyond her professional achievements, Mirzakhani cherished her roles as a wife and mother. In 2005, she married Jan Vondrák, a Czech computer scientist, and in 2011, they welcomed their daughter, Anahita. Friends and colleagues fondly recalled her dedication to her family and her ability to balance the demands of the academic world with her personal commitments (\cite{zagrebnov}).

In 2013, Mirzakhani was diagnosed with breast cancer. Despite her illness, she continued to teach, conduct research, and actively contribute to the mathematical community with remarkable resilience. For several years, she fought the disease while maintaining a spirit of optimism. Tragically, on July 14, 2017, she passed away at the age of forty. Her death was met with profound sorrow across the globe (\cite{zagrebnov,series}).

\subsection{Legacy}
Maryam Mirzakhani's legacy extends far beyond her research contributions. She became a symbol of perseverance and inspiration for women in mathematics and science around the globe (\cite{zagrebnov}). In Iran, her achievements were celebrated as a source of national pride, while internationally, she was recognized as a pioneer who broke through barriers.

Her life is as a powerful reminder that mathematics is not only an abstract discipline but a human endeavor shaped by creativity, imagination, and resilience. She once noted, “The beauty of mathematics only shows itself to more patient followers.” Through her patience and vision, she revealed that beauty to the world (\cite{series}).

Her memory lives on through the many students she mentored, the colleagues who respected her, and the countless young individuals who view her as a model of what can be accomplished through passion and determination. Academic institutions and international societies have established prizes, lectures, and memorials in her honor, ensuring that her influence continues to thrive.

\section{Mathematical Contributions}

This section is not intended to provide full technical details, but rather to outline the main ideas and conceptual innovations in Mirzakhani's contributions, with formal aspects deferred to original sources.

Maryam Mirzakhani's mathematical output is characterized by an ability to synthesize different areas of mathematics, including hyperbolic geometry, topology, and dynamical systems. Rather than focusing on isolated problems, her approach reveals deep structural relationships, often transforming difficult counting questions into geometric problems on moduli spaces. Her early research culminated in her 2007 papers on Weil-Petersson volumes, establishing the foundation for her later work on the moduli spaces of Riemann surfaces (\cite{Mirzakhani2007a, Mirzakhani2007b}). 

This knack for building structural bridges between distinct mathematical domains was recognized in 2014, when she became the first woman to be awarded the Fields Medal. The International Mathematical Union (IMU) stated:
\begin{quote}
For her outstanding contributions to the dynamics and geometry of Riemann surfaces and their moduli spaces (IMU, \citeyear{IMU2014}).
\end{quote}

A unifying theme of her research is the translation of local geometric behaviors—such as the trajectory of a single curve—into global topological invariants. This methodology uncovered connections between pure geometry, topological classification, and mathematical physics.


\subsection{Hyperbolic Geodesics: Beyond the Prime Geodesic Theorem}

The study of geodesics on hyperbolic surfaces is a central topic in differential geometry and ergodic theory. By the Uniformization Theorem, any closed topological surface $S$ of genus $g \geq 2$ admits a hyperbolic metric of constant negative curvature $\kappa = -1$. On such surfaces, the distribution of primitive closed geodesics exhibits chaotic behavior characterized by exponential growth.

In classical dynamical systems, it is well established that the number of all closed geodesics on a hyperbolic surface grows exponentially with respect to their length $L$.\footnote{The Prime Geodesic Theorem states that the number of closed geodesics $\pi(L)$ of length at most $L$ grows asymptotically as $\pi(L) \sim e^{L}/L$. This result is traditionally derived using the Selberg trace formula, establishing a non-trivial analogy with the Prime Number Theorem in number theory, where the exponential growth reflects the high complexity of the geodesic flow.}

Mirzakhani addressed the more constrained and topologically rigid problem of counting \textit{simple} closed geodesics, defined as those without self-intersections.

Counting simple geodesics presents a distinct challenge: as the length $L$ increases, a generic geodesic tends to become equidistributed on the surface, making the "simple" condition statistically rare. Mirzakhani proved a striking contrast with the classical case: while general closed geodesics grow exponentially in number, simple closed geodesics ($s_X(L)$) grow only polynomially. Specifically, she established the following asymptotic limit:
\begin{equation}
    \lim_{L\to\infty} \frac{s_X(L)}{L^{6g-6}} = c_X. 
\end{equation}
where $c_X$ is a constant depending on the geometry of the surface \footnote{The constant $c_X$ depends on the hyperbolic structure of $X$ and is expressed as an explicit Weil-Petersson integral: $c_X = \int_{\mathcal{M}_g} f_X \, \omega_{WP}^{3g-3}$, where $f_X$ is the frequency function introduced in Mirzakhani's 2007 \textit{Inventiones Mathematicae} paper (\cite{Mirzakhani2008}). This identity ties the counting of geodesics on a single surface directly to the volume geometry of the moduli space $\mathcal{M}_g$.} and the exponent $6g-6$ corresponds to the real dimension of the relevant Teichmüller space $\mathcal{T}_g$\footnote{The Teichmüller space $\mathcal{T}_g$ is the space of marked Riemann surfaces of genus $g$. It serves as the universal cover of the moduli space $\mathcal{M}_g$ and, as a real manifold, it is homeomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^{6g-6}$. The fact that the growth exponent of simple geodesics coincides with this dimension is a non-trivial consequence of the symplectic structure of $\mathcal{T}_g$ and the dynamics of the mapping class group.}.
This highlights how imposing a geometric constraint—simplicity—drastically changes the complexity of the problem, revealing an unexpected order within an otherwise chaotic setting.
This result underscores a deep link between the geometry of a single surface and the global structure of the moduli space, showing that counting problems can be understood through averaging processes over families of surfaces.

\subsection{Pants Decomposition and Weil-Petersson Volumes}
A key idea in Mirzakhani's work is to reduce intricate geometric objects to simpler building blocks that can be systematically analyzed.
To analyze the structure of the moduli space $\mathcal{M}_{g,n}$, which parametrizes the possible hyperbolic structures of a surface of genus $g$ with $n$ boundaries, and compute the constants $c_X$, Mirzakhani utilized the topological framework of \textit{pants decomposition}. Any hyperbolic surface $X$ of genus $g$ with $n$ boundary components can be cut along $3g-3+n$ disjoint simple closed geodesics into $2g-2+n$ elementary building blocks, known as \textit{pairs of pants} (spheres with three boundary components). 

This decomposition allows for a "divide and conquer" approach: the problem of calculating the volume of a complex moduli space is broken down into the study of these simpler pieces.
By parameterizing the surface through these blocks, Mirzakhani established a correspondence between the discrete problem of counting geodesics and the continuous evaluation of integrals over $\mathcal{M}_{g,n}$ equipped with the Weil-Petersson metric\footnote{The Weil-Petersson metric is a natural Kähler metric on the moduli space $\mathcal{M}_{g,n}$. For the purposes of this synthesis, its most relevant feature is the associated symplectic form $\omega_{WP}$. The volume $V_{g,n}$ is obtained by integrating the top power of this form over the moduli space: $V_{g,n} = \frac{1}{d!} \int_{\mathcal{M}_{g,n}} \omega_{WP}^d$, where $d = 3g-3+n$ is the complex dimension of $\mathcal{M}_{g,n}$.}. 

The core analytical bridge enabling this topological decomposition and the calculation of volumes is a generalization of the McShane Identity. Mirzakhani's breakthrough was generalizing this identity to surfaces with arbitrary geodesic boundaries and genus\footnote{The generalized identity expresses the length $\ell_1$ of a boundary component as a sum over all possible pairs of pants $P$ containing it. When integrated over the moduli space, this sum transforms into the integration kernel $K(\ell_1, \ell, \ell') = \sum_{\pm} (1 + \exp(\frac{\ell_1 + \ell \pm \ell'}{2}))^{-1}$, which is the "engine" of the recursion (\cite{Mirzakhani2007b}).}.

This leads to a recursive formula where the volume $V_{g,n}(\mathbf{L})$ of $\mathcal{M}_{g,n}$ is expressed in terms of the volumes of moduli spaces of lower complexity. Mirzakhani proved that these volumes are polynomials in the square of the boundary lengths $\ell_i^2$:

\begin{equation}
V_{g,n}(\ell_1, \dots, \ell_n) = \sum_{|\alpha| \le 3g-3+n} C_{\alpha} \ell_1^{2\alpha_1} \dots \ell_n^{2\alpha_n}
\end{equation}

where the coefficients $C_{\alpha}$ are deeply connected to the intersection theory of the moduli space\footnote{The coefficients are precisely the intersection numbers $\langle \tau_{d_1} \dots \tau_{d_n} \rangle$ on $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}$. This links the geometric volume to the algebraic intersection theory used in Witten's conjecture.}.

\subsection{A Geometric Proof of Witten's Conjecture}

Mirzakhani's work on Weil-Petersson volumes also led to a new geometric approach to a central problem in mathematical physics: Witten's conjecture. In 1991, Edward Witten conjectured that the intersection numbers of tautological classes on the Deligne-Mumford compactification of the moduli space, $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}$, are governed by the Korteweg-de Vries (KdV) hierarchy. 

While the first proof was provided by Maxim Kontsevich in 1992 using matrix models and combinatorial methods (\cite{Kontsevich1992}), Mirzakhani developed a purely geometric proof (\cite{Mirzakhani2007b}). Her approach established that the Weil-Petersson volumes $V_{g,n}(L)$ are polynomials in the boundary lengths $L_i^2$, whose coefficients are related to the intersection numbers of $\psi$-classes.\footnote{The relationship is given by the identity: $V_{g,n}(L) = \sum_{|\alpha| \leq 3g-3+n} \frac{\langle \tau_{\alpha_1} \dots \tau_{\alpha_n} \rangle}{\alpha! 2^{|\alpha|}} L_1^{2\alpha_1} \dots L_n^{2\alpha_n}$, where $\langle \tau_{\alpha_1} \dots \tau_{\alpha_n} \rangle$ denote the intersection numbers $\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}} \psi_1^{\alpha_1} \dots \psi_n^{\alpha_n}$.}

Mirzakhani showed that her recursive volume formula is equivalent to the Virasoro constraints. These constraints are known to encode the KdV hierarchy\footnote{By applying a multi-dimensional Laplace transform to the volume polynomials, Mirzakhani obtained a generating function that satisfies the same recursive relations as the tau-function of the KdV hierarchy (\cite{Mirzakhani2007b}).}. This provided a geometric bridge: the KdV hierarchy, once seen as a purely combinatorial or physical property of matrix models, was revealed to be a central consequence of the recursive geometry of hyperbolic surfaces.
The significance of this result lies in its revelation of a profound correspondance between hyperbolic geometru and integrable systems, linking disparate fields such as mathematical physics and pure geometry.

\subsection{Dynamics on Moduli Spaces: The "Magic Wand" Theorem}

In the later stages of her career, Mirzakhani's research shifted from the static geometry of moduli spaces to the dynamics of translation surfaces and rational billiards. This field centers on the ergodic properties of flows on surfaces: whether long-term trajectories equidistribute across the available space or remain confined to potentially complex, fractal sets. 
In collaboration with Alex Eskin and Amir Mohammadi, Mirzakhani addressed the classification of orbit closures under the $SL(2,\mathbb{R})$ action on the moduli space of Abelian differentials\footnote{Abelian differentials are closely related to "translation surfaces"—geometric objects formed by identifying opposite sides of polygons in the plane. This framework allows the translation of the dynamical behavior of a ball in a rational billiard into the study of a flow on a complex surface.}. 
They established a fundamental rigidity theorem, proving that orbit closures are not arbitrary or chaotic sets, but instead exhibit a surprisingly rigid and structured geometric nature. 
In other words, phenomena that initially appear chaotic are in fact governed by hidden geometric constraints.

\begin{theorem}[Eskin-Mirzakhani-Mohammadi, 2015]
Every $SL(2,\mathbb{R})$-orbit closure in the moduli space of Abelian differentials is an affine invariant submanifold.
\end{theorem}

This result, colloquially known as the "Magic Wand" theorem, implies that these orbit closures are sophisticated dynamic objects that behave like algebraic subvarieties, defined locally by linear equations in period coordinates\footnote{The theorem represents an analogue of Ratner's theorems for unipotent flows on homogeneous spaces, but applied to the much more complex setting of non-homogeneous moduli spaces. For the complete proof and its implications for the illumination problem in rational polygons (\cite{Eskin2015}).}. The theorem provides a definitive constraint on the dynamical behavior of translation surfaces, showing that orbit closures have a highly rigid and structured form. It provided major insights on several long-standing problems, including the "illumination problem" in rational polygons, by showing that the movement of a particle (or light ray) in such a system is governed by precise algebraic constraints.

\subsection{Large Genus Asymptotics}

Mirzakhani also investigated the asymptotic behavior of Weil-Petersson volumes as the complexity of the surface—represented by the genus $g$—grows toward infinity. Understanding the large genus limit ($g \to \infty$) is pivotal for establishing connections with statistical mechanics and the thermodynamic limit in quantum gravity models.

She provided precise asymptotic bounds, demonstrating that these volumes exhibit rapid growth characterized by the following relation:
\begin{equation}
    V_g \sim C \cdot (2g)! \cdot (2\pi^2)^{-g} \cdot g^{-1/2}
\end{equation}
where $V_g$ denotes the volume of $\mathcal{M}_{g,0}$ and $C$ is a universal constant\footnote{The precise identification of the constant $C$ and the derivation of the $(2g)!$ growth rate were established in Mirzakhani's 2013 paper in the \textit{Journal of Differential Geometry}. This result allows for the characterization of the statistical properties of "typical" hyperbolic surfaces as the genus becomes arbitrarily large (\cite{Mirzakhani2013}).}.
This asymptotic behavior suggests that, as the complexity of the surface increases, its geometric properties begin to exhibit regular statistical patterns, analogous to phenomena studied in statistical physics.
This research provides a bridge between the enumerative geometry of moduli spaces and the spectral theory of large-order random surfaces.
